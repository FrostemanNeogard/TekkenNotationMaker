Why notation exists

Fighting games are fast, and combos can involve a dozen precise inputs in a couple of seconds. Notation is the community's solution to the problem of sharing those inputs accurately. Rather than filming yourself or writing a paragraph, you compress a combo into a short string that any experienced player can decode and reproduce on their own controller.

The trade-off is that notation is a learned language. Once you understand the handful of conventions on this page, strings that once looked like gibberish become instantly readable, and you can turn them into clear images with the Notation Maker.

Directions and the numpad system

A commonly used notation system for directions is numpad notation. Imagine your keyboard's number pad laid over the joystick or d-pad. Your character is assumed to be on the left, facing right, so "forward" always means toward the opponent.

Input Numpad Tekken letters
Up-back7ub
Up8u
Up-forward9uf
Back4b
Neutral5n
Forward6f
Down-back1db
Down2d
Down-forward3df

In Tekken, capitalization carries meaning: a lowercase f is a quick tap forward, while an uppercase F means you hold forward. This distinction is important for moves that behave differently depending on how long you hold the direction.

Attack buttons

Tekken assigns one button to each limb. Numbers are the universal way to write them because they do not depend on which console someone owns.

Notation Limb PlayStation Xbox
1Left punchSquareX
2Right punchTriangleY
3Left kickXA
4Right kickCircleB

Simultaneous presses are written together. 1+2 means both punches, 3+4 means both kicks, and 1+2+3+4 is all four buttons at once.

Timing and connective symbols

The symbols between inputs are what separate a readable combo from a confusing one. These are the most common:

SymbolMeaning
,Do the next input after the previous one, as a separate press.
~Cancel or link immediately into the next input with tight timing.
+Press these buttons at the same time.
>Wait slightly, then continue. A small deliberate delay.
* or holdHold the input rather than tapping it.
CHCounter hit. The combo requires the first move to counter-hit.
WSWhile standing (see below).

Stances, installs, and while-standing

Many Tekken characters have unique states that change which moves are available. Notation captures these with short prefixes:

  • WS: While standing. The move comes out as your character rises from a crouch, for example after holding down-back and returning to neutral. This stance is often called "while rising", but should not be written as "WR". This is to avoid ambiguity due to the already existing "while running" state, which is notated as "WR".
  • Stances: abbreviations like a character's crouch dash or unique stance precede the button, telling you to enter that state first. The Notation Maker exposes each character's stance icons through the character dropdown.
  • Installs: temporary power-up states are noted where they must be activated in the sequence.

Because these vary per character, selecting the correct character in the editor unlocks the exact icons you need instead of forcing you to type cryptic abbreviations.

Reading a full combo

Put it all together and a combo reads left to right. Take this basic Kazuya combo for example:

f,n,d,df+2, f+2, T!, dash, b+2,2,1

Launch with an EWGF (f,n,d,df+2), perform Tekken 8's combo extension mechanic (Tornado) with forward + right punch , and end off with a dash into a b+2,2,1 string.

The exact abbreviations differ slightly between communities and games, which is precisely why rendering the string as an image with real direction arrows and button icons removes any doubt about what to press.

Tips for writing clear notation

  • Be consistent with capitalization for tap versus hold.
  • Separate deliberate inputs with commas so timing is obvious.
  • Don't forget to note counter-hit requirements.
  • Pick the controller theme your audience actually uses.
