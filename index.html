What is fighting game combo notation?

Combo notation is a shorthand language the fighting game community uses to write down a sequence of inputs so that another player can reproduce it. Instead of describing a combo in full sentences, players write a compact string of directions and attack buttons. A well-written notation tells you exactly what to press, in what order, and often includes timing hints.

There are two common styles: button/icon notation, which uses the actual controller icons for each attack, and numpad notation, which maps the nine directions to the number keys on a keyboard's numpad. This tool can produce both, though is tailored toward the icon style, so you can match whatever your community prefers.

Tekken Notation

Many parts of the Tekken community write directions as relative to the opponent character, meaning you say "forward" instead of "left" (if facing right). These directions as written as shorthand versions of their descriptive names.

Notation Direction
u/b u u/f Up-back / Up / Up-forward
b n f Back / Neutral / Forward
d/b d d/f Down-back / Down / Down-forward

In Tekken specifically you will also see capital letters and symbols: f means tap forward, while F means hold forward. WS means "while standing" (referring to the stance you briefly enter as you rise from crouch, otherwise known as "while rising"), and ~ links inputs that must be done immediately after one another.

Tekken uses a four-button layout, one for each limb. Most communities write these as numbers, which map cleanly to the icons this tool renders:

Notation Limb PlayStation Xbox
1 Left punch Square X
2 Right punch Triangle Y
3 Left kick X A
4 Right kick Circle B

Combined button presses are notated by a "+" sign. For example, 1+2 means both punches at the same time, and 1+2+3+4 presses everything at once.

Numpad notation directions explained

Numpad notation treats your character as if they are always on the left side of the screen, facing right. Each number corresponds to a direction, just like the layout of a numeric keypad:

Numpad Direction
7 8 9 Up-back / Up / Up-forward
4 5 6 Back / Neutral / Forward
1 2 3 Down-back / Down / Down-forward

Reading a full combo, step by step

Once you know the directions and buttons, a combo string reads left to right like a sentence. Consider a combo starter such as an EWGF from Tekken:

f,n,d,df+2

This is the Electric Wind God Fist. You tap forward, return to neutral, tap down, then press down-forward + right punch with tight timing. Each comma marks a separate, deliberate input.

A longer juggle combo simply chains more of these fragments together, often separated by commas or the launcher that starts the sequence. The Notation Maker lets you place each of these pieces visually so the final image is unambiguous, even for players who read a different text convention than you do.

Why use images instead of plain text?

Plain-text notation is compact, but it assumes the reader already knows the convention. Different games and even different communities disagree on symbols, and text can be misread. A rendered image removes ambiguity: the arrows show exactly which direction to hold, and the button icons match what a player sees on their own controller. Images are also far easier to drop into a Discord message, a YouTube thumbnail, a match-up spreadsheet, or a printed practice sheet.

How to use the Notation Maker

  4. Use the cursor arrows and backspace to insert or fix inputs mid-string.
  5. Optionally add top and bottom text, such as the damage value or situation.

Frequently asked questions

Yes. The editor is completely free to use, requires no account, and does not upload your combos anywhere. Image rendering happens locally in your browser.

The tool focuses on the Tekken series, with themes for TEKKEN 8, TEKKEN 7, arcade buttons, and PlayStation/Xbox face buttons, plus a universal numpad style that works for some 2D and 3D fighters.

Can I use the images I make?

You can use exported images for personal use, community content, social media, and strategy guides. Game names, characters, and button designs belong to their respective publishers. See our Terms of Service for details.

What resolution should I export at?

Use high resolution for guides and thumbnails where the image will be enlarged, and medium or low for quick Discord shares where file size matters more than sharpness.
